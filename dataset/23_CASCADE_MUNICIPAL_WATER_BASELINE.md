# Cascade Municipal Water Baseline

> This file establishes the public baseline for Cascade's municipal water system before comparing any proposed industrial load. It does not assume that the proposed Simple Mining facility will use municipal water or any particular volume.
>
> Last reviewed: 2026-09-10

## City-published system baseline

The City of Cascade's Public Works Water Department page currently describes the municipal system as follows:

- **three groundwater wells**;
- two wells drawing from the **Jordan Sandstone Aquifer** and one from the **Silurian Limestone Aquifer**;
- approximately **1,440,000 gallons/day** of well capacity;
- a **400,000-gallon water tower**;
- approximately **230,000 gallons/day** of current municipal water consumption;
- approximately **12 miles** of underground distribution lines.

Those figures provide a useful starting point, but the difference between nominal well capacity and average daily consumption should **not** be treated automatically as available industrial capacity.

## Why nominal spare capacity is not the same as safe new-load capacity

Simple arithmetic using the City-published figures gives roughly 1.21 million gallons/day between stated well capacity and stated average consumption. That number is only a mathematical difference.

A water-system engineer would also need to account for issues such as:

- firm capacity with the largest well unavailable;
- pumping rates and well-specific limitations;
- aquifer sustainable yield;
- treatment capacity;
- storage and pressure requirements;
- peak-day and peak-hour residential/commercial demand;
- fire-flow reserve;
- drought conditions;
- emergency redundancy;
- future community growth;
- distribution-system bottlenecks;
- wastewater treatment capacity;
- the proposed facility's actual peak and annual demand.

Therefore, project approval should rely on a water-capacity study rather than simply subtracting average use from total nameplate well capacity.

## Iowa DNR source-water information

Iowa DNR's Source Water Protection Tracker identifies Cascade Municipal Water Supply as a community groundwater system and provides well/aquifer records. The public tracker lists active, standby, unused, and plugged wells and identifies Cambrian-Ordovician and Silurian sources in the system's history.

Because well status and system configuration can change, the City and DNR's current operating records should control over older historical entries.

## Water quality

Cascade publishes annual Consumer Confidence / Water Quality Reports. The current City page links reports for 2025, 2024, 2023, and earlier years. The 2025 report is the current public report linked from that page as of this dataset review.

A data-center water analysis should distinguish **water quantity/capacity** from **drinking-water quality**. A facility can create a capacity concern without contaminating drinking water, and a water-quality report does not by itself establish how much new industrial demand the system can safely support.

## How this baseline should be used for the Simple Mining proposal

Before assigning a water impact to the proposed facility, obtain:

1. whether municipal water will be used at all;
2. average gallons/day;
3. peak hot-weather gallons/day;
4. annual gallons/year;
5. fire-suppression demand;
6. construction water demand;
7. cooling-system makeup water;
8. sanitary/process wastewater discharge;
9. drought/emergency operating plan;
10. City/utility engineering confirmation that the proposed demand can be served without impairing existing customers or required reserves.

## Comparison with other uses

Earlier project research considered comparing the proposed facility with familiar uses such as greenhouses, irrigation, public facilities, construction, and other local water demands. Such comparisons are only useful when they use the same metric:

- gross withdrawal vs. consumptive use;
- average day vs. peak day;
- annual total;
- potable vs. non-potable water;
- returned wastewater vs. evaporated water;
- seasonal vs. 24/7 demand.

For example, university extension guidance shows that greenhouse peak irrigation demand can be large on a per-acre basis, while a fully dry closed-loop data-center heat-rejection system can use relatively little operational cooling water. Conversely, an evaporatively cooled data center can use much more water. The actual design must control the comparison.
